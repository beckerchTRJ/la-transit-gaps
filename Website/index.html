Los Angeles County

10 Million People, One Sprawling County

A data-driven look at where LA's density meets — and misses — its transit network

Los Angeles County is home to over 10 million residents spread across 4,751 square miles. Some pockets rival Manhattan in density. Vast stretches of desert and mountain hold almost no one. This extreme variation shapes everything about how the county moves.

Population density by census block, 2020 Decennial Census. Each polygon is one of ~91,000 census blocks, dissolved into quantile bins for rendering. No basemap — just the geometry of where people live.

The densest blocks — in Koreatown, Westlake, and East Hollywood — exceed 100,000 persons per square mile, rivaling the densest neighborhoods in New York City. Meanwhile, the northern third of the county — the Antelope Valley, Angeles National Forest — barely registers on the scale.

This density map is the foundation of our analysis. In a well-connected transit system, service should roughly follow density: more people, more transit. But does it?

Coverage Analysis

How Far Is the Nearest Stop?

Transit planners use walking-distance buffers to measure coverage. The standard thresholds: a ¼-mile walk (~5 minutes) to a bus stop, and a ½-mile walk (~10 minutes) to a rail station. These distances come from FTA and TCRP transit planning research.

Left: ¼-mile walking buffer around all bus stops (Metro Bus + 13 municipal agencies). Right: ½-mile buffer around rail stations (Metro Rail + Metrolink). Orange/blue = covered area within LA County boundary.

LA's bus network blankets the urbanized area — the vast majority of city residents live within a short walk of a bus stop. But bus service varies wildly in frequency and reliability.

Rail tells a different story. Despite billions invested in Metro Rail expansion over the past three decades, the rail network's geographic reach remains limited. Only a fraction of residents can walk to a rail station, concentrating high-quality transit access in a few corridors.

Gap Analysis

Transit Deserts — Where Density Meets Neglect

Not all gaps are equal. A low-density exurb with no rail isn't a "transit desert" — it's expected. The real gaps are places where density is high but transit access is low. To find them, we compute a distance-decayed access score for each hex — nearby stops contribute more than distant ones — then rank each hex against its density peers using population quintiles.

Red hexes score in the bottom half of their density peer group. Blue hexes are better-served than average. The deficit ranges from −0.5 (worst in its density tier) to +0.5 (best in its density tier), with 0 = median peer.

Transit access deficit by H3 hex (resolution 8, ~0.7 km²). Color = percentile rank within density quintile minus 0.5. Only hexes with >1,000 persons/mi² shown.

The deepest transit deserts cluster in South LA, the eastern San Fernando Valley, and parts of the San Gabriel Valley — areas with significant density but limited rail access and sometimes sparse bus service. These neighborhoods depend on transit but are poorly served by it.

What Would Good Look Like?

If you could place new rail stations anywhere in the county, where would they do the most good? We used a greedy set-cover algorithm: iteratively place a station at the densest location without rail, covering all residents within 1 km, then repeat.
